# Research Evidence Supporting Features

## 1. Capture a Response

### Probe Evidence

Interactive activities received the highest number of responses. This suggests that visitors prefer active participation rather than passive viewing.

### Interview Evidence

> “The best part was probably seeing my child actually engaged, especially when there was something hands-on to do instead of just walking around looking at artworks.”  
> — Elaris_3

> “If there’s something I can walk around, interact with, or respond to, it feels easier to get involved.”  
> — minngjie xi

### Literature Evidence

**Source:** _Protocol to Build a Framework for Designing an Interactive Tool for Art Gallery Visitors_

> “Gallery visitors are more akin to active explorers and meaning makers than passive receivers of content.”

This supports a feature that allows visitors to actively record their observations, feelings, and interpretations of artworks.

---

## 2. Shared Visitor Traces

### Probe Evidence

Ways to interact with others received a high number of responses, showing visitors’ interest in social interaction during gallery visits.

### Interview Evidence

> “After I finished my sketch, I kind of wished I had someone to show it to. It wasn't very good, but I still wanted to laugh about it with someone or see how they would draw the same thing.”  
> — Elaris_2

> “I kind of wanted to ask her for some tips, but... I don't know, I felt a bit awkward just talking to a stranger.”  
> — Elaris_2

### Literature Evidence

**Source:** _The Silent Conversation: Designing for Introspection and Social Play in Art Museums_

> “Players used the app for communicating with each other during the visit, often without speaking.”

This supports anonymous and non-verbal visitor traces that allow people to interact without directly speaking to strangers.

---

## 3. Gallery Map

### Probe Evidence

Clear routes and maps or guides received noticeable interest. One participant also suggested:

> “Visitor route recommendation.”

### Interview Evidence

> “The route wasn't super clear, so sometimes we ended up walking through the same area again.”  
> — Elaris_1

> “We kind of ended up doing loops through the same rooms because we couldn't tell if we'd already seen everything.”  
> — Elaris_3

### Literature Evidence

**Source:** _Flow, Staging, Wayfinding, Personalization: Evaluating User Experience with Mobile Museum Narratives_

The study highlights the importance of visitor control and flexible mobile guidance for different visitor needs.

This supports a gallery map that shows visited, unvisited, and temporarily skipped areas without forcing visitors to follow a fixed route.

---

## 4. My Collection

### Probe Evidence

Ways to save favourites received a high number of responses, showing that visitors want to keep meaningful content from their visits.

### Interview Evidence

> “We’d stop when one of us saw something interesting and talk about it a little. I also took a few photos, mostly of artworks that looked unusual or that I wanted to remember.”  
> — Participant 1, Interview Excerpt 1

> “After I finished my sketch, I kind of wished I had someone to show it to.”  
> — Elaris_2

### Literature Evidence

**Source:** _Sensitive Pictures: Emotional Interpretation in the Museum_

> “Presented these back to visitors as data souvenirs.”

The study suggests that data collected during a museum visit can become a personalised souvenir that supports reflection after the visit.

This supports My Collection, where visitors can save artworks, personal responses, and visual traces for later review.

# Prototype Features Supported by Two Evidence Sources

## 5. Visual Footprint

### Probe Evidence

Colours and visuals received a high number of responses. This suggests that visual elements are important for attracting visitors’ attention and presenting their experiences.

### Literature Evidence

**Source:** _Learning from Interactive Museum Installations: About Interaction Design for Public Settings_

> “The content of installations can consist of the users’ very own activity.”

This suggests that visitors’ responses and actions can become content that other people encounter.

**Source:** _Sensitive Pictures: Emotional Interpretation in the Museum_

> “Systems should support human users in understanding, interpreting, and experiencing emotion in its full complexity and ambiguity.”

This supports transforming a visitor’s written response into an abstract visual footprint. The generated represents a creative interpretation rather than an objective measurement of emotion.

---

## 6. Discover

### Interview Evidence

> “Seeing them stay there for so long made me curious. So I kind of went back to look at it again.”  
> — Elaris_1

> “My child immediately wanted to try it too, so we headed over to the drawing area. I probably wouldn't even have noticed it if other people weren't already doing it.”  
> — Elaris_3

> “One person was taking a photo from really far away. Then I went to the same spot and… actually, the artwork looked better from there.”  
> — Interview Participant

### Literature Evidence

**Source:** _Social Augmented Reality: Communicating via Cultural Heritage_

> “Even lone visitors pay attention to what other visitors do during the visit.”

> “They are influenced by the behaviour of other visitors.”

This supports a Discover feature that makes other visitors’ attention and interpretations subtly visible. These traces may help visitors notice artworks or details they could otherwise miss, without directly recommending a fixed route.

## 7. Optional Personalised Route

### Probe Evidence

Personalised gallery routes received a moderate level of interest. Clear routes and maps or guides also received many responses, showing a need for better navigation support.

### Interview Evidence

> “The route wasn't super clear, so sometimes we ended up walking through the same area again.”  
> — Elaris_1

> “ avertisementWe kind of ended up doing loops through the same rooms because we couldn't tell if we'd already seen everything.”  
> — Elaris_3

> “I definitely wouldn’t want something that tells me exactly where to go or keeps giving me notifications. I’d still want to feel like I’m exploring the gallery myself.”  
> — minngjie xi

### Feature Connection

The feature could suggest routes based on visitors’ interests, saved artworks, available time, or previously visited areas. Visitors would remain free to ignore or change the suggested route.

### Reason for Not Implementing

Gallery exhibitions and artwork locations change regularly, so the system would require continuously updated exhibition and location data.

Route recommendations could also direct visitors towards selected artworks while causing them to miss other valuable works. This may reduce independent exploration and give the system too much control over the gallery experience.

**Prototype status:** Not implemented.

## 8. Layered Artwork Information

### Interview Evidence

> “After a few artworks I just wanted to look, not keep listening.”  
> — Yifan_2

> “Maybe just basic information first, and if I’m interested, I can choose to know more.”  
> — Yifan_3

### Literature Evidence

**Source:** _Flow, Staging, Wayfinding, Personalization: Evaluating User Experience with Mobile Museum Narratives_

> “Visitors prefer short, layered segments of audio, as it puts control into the hands of the user.”

### Feature Connection

The feature could present basic artwork information first and allow visitors to open additional details when needed. This would give visitors control over how much information they receive.

### Reason for Not Implementing

The feature would require accurate and approved information for every artwork. Producing and maintaining this content would depend on access to the gallery’s collection database and curatorial information.

The current prototype focuses on visitor-created responses and social interaction. Adding extensive artwork information could increase screen time and distract visitors from the physical artworks.

**Prototype status:** Not implemented.

---

## 9. Artwork Observation Challenges

### Probe Evidence

Observation challenges received a moderate number of responses, showing interest in activities that encourage closer observation.

### Interview Evidence

> “Once I started drawing, I realised the dark areas actually had lots of different strokes and layers. I probably wouldn't have noticed that if I was just walking past.”  
> — Elaris_2

> “Sometimes I’d look at a painting for a few seconds and think, ‘Okay… now what?’ I didn’t really know whether there was something specific I was meant to notice.”  
> — minngjie xi

### Feature Connection

The feature could provide optional prompts that ask visitors to find a particular colour, shape, detail, or viewing position within an artwork.

### Reason for Not Implementing

Challenges focused on small visual details might encourage visitors to move too close to artworks. This could increase the risk of touching artworks, crossing safety boundaries, obstructing other visitors, or creating crowding around fragile paintings and prints.

Predefined observation tasks could also influence what visitors look for and limit their personal interpretations. This would conflict with the project’s goal of supporting open and independent exploration.

**Prototype status:** Not implemented.

## 10. Translation Support

### Probe Evidence

One participant directly identified the lack of translation:

> “No translation.”

### Interview Evidence

> “But the font was... it was really small. Really, really small. So sometimes I had to get quite close to read it, and even then it wasn't very easy.”  
> — Fu_3

> “Maybe just basic information first, and if I’m interested, I can choose to know more.”  
> — Yifan_3

### Feature Connection

The feature could allow visitors to select a preferred language when viewing artwork descriptions and other gallery information.

### Reason for Not Implementing

Reliable translation requires accurate source information and review across multiple languages. Automatic translation could misrepresent artwork titles, cultural concepts, artist statements, or specialised terminology.

Translation is valuable for accessibility, but it is outside the scope of the current prototype. A future version should use translations supplied or reviewed by the gallery.

**Prototype status:** Not implemented.

## 11. Digital Sketching

### Probe Evidence

Interactive activities received the highest number of responses. This shows that visitors are interested in active and creative participation during gallery visits.

### Interview Evidence

> “Once I started drawing, I realised the dark areas actually had lots of different strokes and layers. I probably wouldn't have noticed that if I was just walking past.”  
> — Elaris_2

> “After I finished my sketch, I kind of wished I had someone to show it to. It wasn't very good, but I still wanted to laugh about it with someone or see how they would draw the same thing.”  
> — Elaris_2

### Literature Evidence

**Source:** _Learning from Interactive Museum Installations: About Interaction Design for Public Settings_

> “The content of installations can consist of the users’ very own activity.”

### Feature Connection

Visitors could create or upload sketches connected to specific artworks. They could also browse and compare how other visitors represented the same artwork.

### Reason for Not Implementing

A complete drawing interface would require additional tools, such as brushes, colours, erasers, image uploading, and content moderation. It could also overlap with the current Capture a Response and Visual Footprint features.

**Prototype status:** Not implemented.

Visitors could control how much social information they see. They could temporarily hide visitor traces, reduce recommendations, or enter a quiet viewing mode.

### Reason for Not Implementing

**Prototype status:** Not implemented as a separate feature.

## 13. Visited and Skipped Area Tracking

### Probe Evidence

Clear routes and maps or guides received a high number of responses, showing that visitors need support understanding where they have already been.

### Interview Evidence

> “The route wasn't super clear, so sometimes we ended up walking through the same area again.”  
> — Elaris_1

> “We kind of ended up doing loops through the same rooms because we couldn't tell if we'd already seen everything.”  
> — Elaris_3

### Feature Connection

The Gallery Map could display three area states:

- **Visited:** areas the visitor has already explored.
- **Unvisited:** areas the visitor has not entered.
- **Skipped:** crowded or unavailable areas the visitor may want to revisit.

This would reduce repeated walking without forcing visitors to follow a fixed route.

### Implementation Limitation

Accurate area tracking would require indoor location data or visitors manually marking areas as visited. The current prototype can demonstrate the interface states, but it cannot automatically detect a visitor’s real location.

**Prototype status:** Partially represented in the Gallery Map.

---

## 14. Return Later Reminder

### Interview Evidence

> “If it looks really crowded, I don't want to squeeze in or stand behind people waiting. I might think I’ll come back later, but sometimes I probably forget.”  
> — minngjie xi

### Peer Feedback Evidence

Feedback from the previous presentation suggested using temporary detours and guiding visitors back to skipped areas later. This could reduce crowding without removing parts of the exhibition from the visitor’s experience.

### Feature Connection

Visitors could mark an artwork or gallery area as “Return Later.” The system would save it to the Gallery Map or My Collection and provide one optional reminder before the visitor leaves.

### Reason for Not Implementing

A real-time reminder would require location tracking, crowd information, or reliable detection of when a visitor is preparing to leave. Frequent reminders could also distract visitors from artworks.

A simplified version could allow visitors to manually save skipped artworks without using automatic location tracking.

**Prototype status:** Not implemented.
