# Research Methods and Rationale

Our research used three connected methods: **literature research, an in-situ participatory probe, and interviews**. These methods were selected to move progressively from understanding the existing research space, to identifying patterns in real gallery experiences, and finally to explaining the reasons behind those patterns.

## 1. Literature Research

We first reviewed previous HCI and museum-related studies about gallery experiences, social interaction, mobile technologies, and interactive systems.

The purpose of the literature research was to understand what kinds of technological interventions had already been explored and, more importantly, what aspects of the gallery experience appeared under-supported. We were particularly interested in whether existing approaches could be extended through **social and mobile qualities**, such as visitor-to-visitor influence, context-aware interaction, mobility through space, and lightweight interaction that does not distract from artworks.

This stage helped us identify several areas worth investigating further, including how visitors notice artworks, how other people influence attention and interpretation, and how technology might support social connection without dominating the gallery experience.

## 2. In-situ Participatory Probe

We then used a physical sticker-based probe in the gallery to collect quick responses from visitors about their immediate experience.

The probe asked participants about five areas:

- What caught their attention during the visit
- How they preferred to explore the gallery
- What made them stay longer
- Which kinds of interaction they would like to see more of
- Any additional needs or suggestions

Participants could select options such as **artworks, stories and meaning, colours and visuals, space and atmosphere, wandering freely, exploring with others, interactive activities, personalised routes, saving favourites, and ways to interact with others**.

This method was chosen because it allowed us to gather a relatively broad range of responses directly in the gallery context without requiring participants to complete a long interview. The visual and low-effort format also made it easier to identify recurring patterns across visitors.

More importantly, the probe helped us examine whether the areas suggested by the literature were actually visible in visitors' real experiences. It showed that gallery experience was not only about viewing artworks: visitors also cared about **exploration style, social interaction, atmosphere, recommendations, interactivity, and ways of keeping or sharing parts of the experience**.

The structure of the probe also followed a progression from experience to possible intervention:

**Attention → Behaviour → Engagement → Design Opportunity**

This allowed us to first understand what visitors noticed, how they explored, and what sustained their engagement before asking what kinds of interaction they might want to try.

## 3. Interview

After identifying broader patterns through the probe, we conducted interviews to explore the experiences behind those patterns in more depth.

The interviews focused on participants' previous gallery visits, including what they did, what made them stop at particular artworks, how companions affected their experience, whether other visitors influenced what they noticed, and when they preferred social interaction versus independent exploration.

The interview method was important because the probe could show **what people selected**, but it could not fully explain **why they selected it**.

For example, selecting "explore with others" does not reveal whether the value comes from conversation, reassurance, discovering details through another person, or simply companionship. Similarly, selecting "interactive activities" does not explain whether interaction supports understanding, attention, enjoyment, or social connection.

The interviews therefore helped uncover the meanings behind the behavioural patterns identified in the probe.

## How the Methods Worked Together

The three methods supported different levels of understanding:

**Literature Research**  
→ What has already been explored, and where are possible gaps or opportunities?

**Probe**  
→ What patterns actually appear in visitors' gallery experiences?

**Interview**  
→ Why do these patterns happen, and what do they mean to visitors?

**Synthesis**  
→ What recurring needs, behaviours, and design opportunities emerge across the different sources?

**Concept Development**  
→ How can social and mobile technology respond to these opportunities?

Together, these methods allowed us to triangulate findings rather than relying only on previous research or user opinions.

The combined research suggested that gallery experience is shaped not only by artworks, but also by **other people, spatial context, movement, attention, interpretation, and personal exploration preferences**. It also suggested that visitors value social influence and shared experience while still wanting to maintain their own pace and independence.

These findings then informed our later design direction: exploring how a **social and mobile system could allow visitors to become aware of, receive, and leave traces of other people's experiences without forcing direct interaction or interrupting engagement with the gallery itself**.
