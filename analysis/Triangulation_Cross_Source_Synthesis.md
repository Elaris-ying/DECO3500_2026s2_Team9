*Literature Analysis + Interview Analysis + Culture Probe Analysis*

# 1. Purpose of the Triangulation

This synthesis brings together three separate evidence streams: the paper-based literature analysis, the interview analysis, and the culture probe analysis. The purpose is to identify where the sources converge, complement one another, extend prior research, or diverge. The synthesis does not replace the individual analyses; instead, it uses them together to establish the strongest cross-source insights and to show which design directions are supported by more than one form of evidence.

# 3. Triangulation Logic

Each cross-source finding was assessed using four relationships:

-   Convergence - two or more sources independently support the same underlying idea.

-   Complementarity - the sources support the same area but contribute different aspects or levels of detail.

-   Extension - the primary research adds a more specific mechanism, nuance, or contextual condition beyond the literature.

-   Divergence - the sources point in different directions or reveal a tension that should not be collapsed into one conclusion.

# 5. Integrated Cross-Source Themes

## Theme 1 - Gallery engagement is socially shaped, even when interaction is indirect

Across the three evidence streams, gallery experience is not purely individual. Prior research emphasises co-presence, shared interpretation and social meaning-making; interviews show that both companions and unfamiliar visitors redirect attention and influence interpretation; and the culture probe shows strong interest in exploring and interacting with others. The combined evidence suggests that sociality includes conversation, collaboration, observation and subtle awareness of other people.

**Design implication:** Support social awareness and shared discovery without assuming that visitors want constant conversation or direct messaging.

## Theme 2 - Visitors want connection without surrendering autonomy

The literature repeatedly emphasises pace, choice and optional communication. Interviews show that companions can enrich a visit but also create pressure to keep moving. The culture probe similarly shows preferences for both social exploration and individual control. The core need is therefore not simply more social interaction, but the ability to move between togetherness and independence.

**Design implication:** Support asynchronous and optional social interaction, flexible pacing and independent exploration.

## Theme 3 - Active participation is a stronger engagement mechanism than passive information delivery

Hands-on, playful and embodied activities are consistently valued in the literature. Interview participants describe sketching and interactive activity as reasons to look more carefully. The culture probe strongly favours interactive activities as something that encourages visitors to stay longer. This is one of the strongest points of convergence across all sources.

**Design implication:** Prioritise look-do-look-again activities, observation prompts, making, challenges and embodied interaction over information-heavy features.

## Theme 4 - Guidance should enable discovery rather than prescribe meaning

Prior research supports progressive disclosure and visitor control over information. Interviewees want context but reject overload and authoritative explanations. The culture probe shows that stories and guidance matter, but visitors also want autonomy and interest-led exploration. Guidance is therefore most useful when it helps visitors enter the experience without narrowing interpretation.

**Design implication:** Use short prompts and optional contextual depth; avoid fixed routes, excessive recommendations and authoritative interpretations.

## Theme 5 - Technology should recede into the background of the gallery experience

The literature warns that digital systems can isolate visitors and divert attention from exhibits. Interviews explicitly reject screen-heavy experiences and constant notifications. The culture probe shows weaker interest in sound/media than in interaction and sociality. Together, the evidence supports technology that briefly facilitates engagement and then returns attention to artworks, people and space.

**Design implication:** Keep interactions lightweight, peripheral, context-sensitive and physically oriented.

## Theme 6 - The gallery environment is part of the interaction system

Across the literature, interviews and probe, engagement is shaped by spatial arrangement, navigation, crowding, atmosphere, seating, noise and visibility. These factors determine what visitors can see, how long they stay and whether social interaction feels possible.

**Design implication:** Treat context such as crowding, location, visibility, comfort and movement as core design variables rather than background conditions.

# 6. Findings That Extend the Literature

The triangulation also identifies several contributions that are more specific in the primary research than in the literature:

-   Other visitors function as an informal guidance layer. Interviews show that where people stand, how long they look, whether they sketch, and how they position themselves can redirect attention without direct communication.

-   Social behaviour can provide permission to participate. Seeing someone else sketch or interact can signal that a behaviour is acceptable and reduce uncertainty.

-   Social activity has a threshold effect. Moderate visible activity can attract attention, while excessive crowding can repel visitors.

-   Seating can be engagement infrastructure, not only a rest facility. It can support longer looking, sketching and conversation.

-   Creating something can bridge private and social engagement. Making or sketching may begin as an individual activity but create a later desire to compare, share or discuss.

# 8. Cross-Source User Needs

1.  Visitors need opportunities to discover interesting artworks through social and contextual cues without having their route prescribed.

2.  Visitors need to encounter perspectives beyond their own while retaining ownership of interpretation.

3.  Visitors need control over pace, sequence and when they engage socially.

4.  Visitors need lightweight ways to reconnect and share discoveries after exploring independently.

5.  Visitors need active, participatory reasons to look more carefully rather than simply more information.

6.  Visitors need guidance that is concise, optional and available at the moment it becomes useful.

7.  Visitors need social features that do not require direct communication with strangers.

8.  Visitors need technology that preserves attention to artworks, companions and the physical gallery.

9.  Visitors need interaction that accounts for spatial and practical conditions such as crowding, navigation, seating and atmosphere.

10. Visitors need adaptable options because motivations, confidence, information preferences and social preferences differ.

# 9. Cross-Source Design Principles

-   Preserve visitor agency: allow control over pace, route, information depth and social participation.

-   Support social awareness before adding direct communication features.

-   Enable asynchronous sharing so visitors can separate and reconnect without losing the shared experience.

-   Prompt active observation and participation rather than relying on passive content delivery.

-   Use progressive disclosure: small cue first, deeper context only when requested.

-   Redirect attention toward artworks and the physical environment after every digital intervention.

-   Do not equate popularity with value; account for crowding and contextual conditions.

-   Offer adaptable interaction styles rather than a single fixed experience.

-   Frame interpretive or system-generated outputs as prompts and perspectives rather than objective truth.

-   Design for the whole situated gallery system, including movement, seating, visibility, noise, proximity and atmosphere.

# 10. Integrated Design Opportunities

## Opportunity 1 - Reveal lightweight social traces

Make subtle social activity visible, such as what others noticed, where they looked from, or that someone returned to an artwork, without turning this into a popularity leaderboard.

## Opportunity 2 - Support asynchronous group exploration

Allow companions to separate, leave lightweight reactions or observations, and encounter one another's discoveries later.

## Opportunity 3 - Prompt deeper observation

Use small activities such as looking from another position, finding a detail, sketching, comparing, or responding to a question.

## Opportunity 4 - Provide optional contextual depth

Offer a brief cue or interpretation first, followed by deeper information only when the visitor chooses.

## Opportunity 5 - Design for context-sensitive engagement

Consider crowding, available space, seating, movement and atmosphere when determining when and how an interaction should appear.

# 11. Integrated Core Insight

**Gallery experiences are shaped by the interaction between artworks, visitors, other people, the physical environment and mediating technology. Across the literature, interviews and culture probe, the strongest opportunity is not to add more information or more communication, but to support active and social engagement while preserving visitor autonomy and attention to the physical gallery. Visitors benefit from other people's presence, perspectives and activity, yet they also need control over pace, interpretation and participation. This points toward lightweight, context-sensitive technology that helps visitors notice, act, share and reconnect without becoming the centre of the experience.**

**Integrated design question:** How might we make the social activity, perspectives and discoveries already present within galleries more visible and useful, while preserving visitors' independence, attention and freedom to explore?

# 12. Interpretation Boundary

The triangulation strengthens conclusions that are supported across multiple sources, but it does not justify assuming a particular technology solution. The combined evidence supports sociality, active participation, optional guidance and contextual awareness, but does not by itself establish that visitors want a chat application, AI-generated interpretation, stranger messaging, a compulsory personalised route, or any other specific implementation. Those decisions should be evaluated in the next concept-development and testing stage.
