# Interview Analysis 

We conducted a thematic analysis of the interview data to identify recurring patterns across participants. The interview transcripts were first reviewed and coded to capture relevant behaviours, needs, attitudes, and experiences. Related codes were then grouped using affinity diagramming, which supported the identification of broader themes across the dataset. These themes were subsequently interpreted to identify key user needs, tensions, and design opportunities relevant to the development of our concept.

## 1. Familiarisation

Across the transcripts, participants describe gallery visits as a combination of:

-   looking at artworks;

-   talking with friends;

-   observing other visitors;

-   sketching or interacting with exhibits;

-   reading/listening to contextual information;

-   managing their own pace;

-   dealing with crowding, fatigue, seating and the physical environment.

A particularly strong pattern appears across the dataset: gallery engagement is socially influenced even when visitors do not directly interact with each other. Visitors watch where others stand, what they look at, how they interact, and what they say.

At the same time, participants repeatedly value having control over their own pace.

# 3. Clustering the Codes

Codes:

-   friend points out unnoticed detail;

-   following others’ attention;

-   learning by observing others;

-   overhearing creates new understanding;

-   social behaviour legitimises participation;

-   crowds attract attention.

These codes share one underlying idea:

Other people’s behaviour provides information about how and where to engage.

### Cluster B — Social interpretation

Codes:

-   different perspectives deepen interpretation;

-   conversation encourages looking again;

-   shared confusion is enjoyable;

-   friends help interpret unfamiliar work;

-   discussing artwork increases attention.

Underlying idea:

Other people’s perspectives change how visitors understand and experience artworks.

### Cluster C — Autonomy versus togetherness

Codes:

-   group pace creates pressure;

-   preference for individual pace;

-   separate then reconnect;

-   wanting quiet attention;

-   wanting to share discoveries afterwards.

Underlying idea:

Visitors value social connection but do not want it to control their exploration.

-   interactive works encourage engagement;

-   doing something creates closer observation;

-   interaction reduces uncertainty about how to engage;

# 4. Final Themes

## Theme 1 — Social cues guide attention and discovery

One of the strongest patterns across the interviews is that visitors use other people as informal sources of information.

This can happen without direct communication.

Participants described:

-   following friends who stopped at particular artworks;

-   noticing artworks because several people were looking at them;

-   copying another visitor’s viewing position;

-   watching others before trying interactive installations;

-   seeing another visitor sketch and realising sketching was acceptable;

-   overhearing comments and subsequently returning to artworks.

For example, one participant had already walked past a painting but returned because another visitor had been sketching it for a long time:

“Seeing them stay there for so long made me curious.”

Another participant saw someone taking a photograph from far away, moved to the same position, and discovered that:

“the artwork looked better from there.”

### Interpretation

Visitors are not simply looking at artworks.

They are also continuously interpreting **the behaviour of other visitors**.

We could therefore describe social behaviour as an:

**informal navigation and recommendation system within the gallery.**

### Design implication

The system could make useful social cues more visible without requiring visitors to communicate directly.

For example:

-   “Several visitors noticed this from another angle.”

-   “Someone spent time looking at this detail.”

-   “Other visitors explored this from both sides.”

The important point is that the technology should **support discovery rather than tell visitors what they must see**.

# Theme 2 — Shared perspectives deepen engagement

Participants consistently described other people’s perspectives as adding something to their own experience.

Friends:

-   notice different details;

-   interpret abstract works differently;

-   provide knowledge participants do not have;

-   create conversation around confusing artworks.

Fu_3 gives a particularly strong example.

The participant was interested primarily in **cultural stories**, while their illustrator friend noticed **colour, character design and visual details**.

The participant described the combination as making the experience feel:

“more complete.”

Similarly, another participant explained that disagreement was actually productive:

“disagreeing can actually make it more interesting.”

Because disagreement caused them to look again.

The interaction often followed:

**Look → share → reconsider → look again → notice more**

### Interpretation

Conversation does not simply make the gallery “more social”.

It actively changes **how visitors see the artwork**.

### Design implication

A system should therefore avoid providing only authoritative interpretations.

Instead of:

**“This artwork represents X.”**

it could encourage perspectives:

**“Someone else noticed the contrast between these two areas. What do you notice?”**

This maintains visitor interpretation rather than replacing it.

# Theme 3 — Visitors want connection without losing control of their own pace

This is probably the most important **tension** in the dataset.

Participants clearly value being with other people because of:

-   conversation;

-   humour;

-   different interpretations;

-   shared discovery;

-   interactive experiences.

But the same social experience creates pressure.

Several participants essentially said:

“I don’t want to make them wait.”

When friends move faster, participants:

-   stop reading;

-   shorten their observation;

-   abandon sketches;

-   move to another area;

-   feel pressure to catch up.

Going alone provides:

**control over pace.**

Fu_3 even described an ideal arrangement:

**explore individually first → meet afterwards → discuss**

Mingjie Participant 2 expressed almost exactly the same preference.

### Interpretation

Therefore, the user need is **not**:

Visitors want a more social gallery.

It is much more nuanced:

**Visitors want opportunities for social connection while retaining autonomy over when and how that connection happens.**

### Design implication

The system should support **asynchronous social interaction**.

For example:

Someone in the group discovers something interesting → leaves a lightweight marker → their friend encounters it later → decides whether to explore it.

That is more consistent with your findings than forcing the group to remain together.

# Theme 4 — Active participation creates deeper looking

Sketching provides particularly strong evidence for this theme.

Across Elaris and Yifan participants, the same pattern appears repeatedly.

Without drawing:

**look → move on**

With drawing:

**look → draw → compare → notice detail → continue looking**

One participant said:

“When I draw, I have to actually… look properly.”

Participants reported noticing:

-   brushwork;

-   shadows;

-   composition;

-   spacing;

-   hand positions;

-   layers;

-   small visual details.

Interactive installations also provided casual visitors with an easier way into the experience.

Mingjie Participant 3 explained why:

“I don’t have to figure out how I’m supposed to engage with it.”

### Interpretation

This suggests an important distinction:

**People may not need more information. They may need a reason to look.**

### Design implication

Instead of giving users more content to consume, your technology could give them small observational actions.

For example:

-   “Find one detail your friend may have missed.”

-   “Look again from another position.”

-   “Choose the part you would sketch.”

-   “What changed after looking for 20 seconds?”

-   “What is the first thing you noticed?”

The interface becomes a **prompt for engagement**, rather than the activity itself.

# Theme 5 — Guidance should support rather than compete with exploration

There is another tension between:

**wanting context**

and

**wanting freedom to explore.**

Context can significantly improve engagement.

Fu_3 read the mythology surrounding the sculptures and then returned to them with a different understanding.

Mingjie Participant 2 also returned to an initially overlooked work after reading a short description.

But participants also described:

-   labels being too long;

-   text being too small;

-   audio containing too much information;

-   not wanting explanations everywhere;

-   not wanting constant notifications;

-   not wanting to continuously look at their phone.

Yifan_3 captured the preferred structure particularly clearly:

“Maybe just basic information first, and if I’m interested, I can choose to know more.”

### Interpretation

Visitors don’t necessarily reject information.

They want **control over when and how deeply they engage with it**.

### Design implication

Use **progressive disclosure**:

**Small cue → visitor chooses → deeper information**

rather than:

**Large amount of information → visitor has to filter it**

This also applies to social information.

# 5. Cross-cutting contextual finding

## Physical and social conditions determine whether engagement is possible

Participants mentioned:

-   lack of seating;

-   uncomfortable benches;

-   fatigue;

-   crowding;

-   noise;

-   darkness;

-   confusing routes;

-   slippery floors;

-   lack of space.

Importantly, these aren’t simply “gallery complaints”.

They affect the behaviours you’re interested in.

For example:

**No seating → less sketching**

**Crowding → visitor skips artwork**

**Quiet space → longer observation**

**Seating near artwork → longer viewing + conversation**

**Dark/noisy environment → reduced comfort and attention**

So when designing a **social and contextual technology**, the word **contextual** becomes very important.

The system should not assume that every visitor is equally ready to interact at every moment.

# 8. Design Opportunities

### Opportunity 1: Reveal social traces

Allow visitors to see subtle traces of what others noticed, explored or found interesting.

Not:

**“This is the most popular artwork.”**

More like:

**“Someone noticed something unusual here.”**

### Opportunity 2: Support asynchronous group exploration

Friends should be able to temporarily separate without losing the shared experience.

For example:

**People notices artwork → leaves reaction/prompt → friend encounters it later → responds → they discuss afterwards.**

### Opportunity 3: Prompt deeper observation

Use very short activities that redirect attention toward the physical artwork.

**Technology → prompt → artwork**

rather than:

**Artwork → technology → technology → technology**

### Opportunity 4: Provide optional contextual depth

Users should decide whether they want:

**quick cue**

→ **visitor perspective**

→ **background/context**

→ **deeper information**

rather than receiving everything immediately.

# 9. Core Insight

**Gallery experiences are shaped not only by artworks but also by the visible and invisible presence of other visitors. People use others’ attention, behaviour, and interpretations to discover and understand artworks, yet they also value maintaining control over their own pace and attention. This creates an opportunity for lightweight technology that makes useful social cues and perspectives visible without requiring constant interaction or distracting visitors from the physical gallery experience.**

One very strong design question

**How might we make the social activity already occurring within galleries more visible and useful, while preserving visitors’ independence, attention, and freedom to explore?**
