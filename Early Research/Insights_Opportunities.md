# Insights

## 1. Visitors Need Low-Pressure Social Connection

Visitors want to understand and share other people’s responses, but direct interaction with strangers can feel awkward. Social interaction should therefore be anonymous, non-verbal, asynchronous, and optional.

## 2. Personal Interpretations Can Become Shared Content

Visitors do more than receive official gallery information. They also form their own observations, emotions, and interpretations. These personal responses can be recorded and presented as content for other visitors to discover.

## 3. Visual Expression Can Lower the Interaction Barrier

Visual content can attract attention without requiring visitors to write or read long messages. Transforming written responses into abstract visuals could provide a lightweight way to express and share personal interpretations.

## 4. Other Visitors Can Support Discovery

Visitors can be influenced by where other people stop, take photographs, sketch, or discuss artworks. These behaviours can become subtle discovery cues, but they should not be presented as instructions that visitors must follow.

## 5. Guidance Must Preserve Visitor Autonomy

Visitors need maps and route support to reduce repeated walking and missed areas. However, they also want to explore at their own pace. Navigation should provide optional support without forcing visitors to follow a fixed route.

## 6. Mobile Interaction Should Remain Optional and Lightweight

Mobile technology can support reflection, social connection, and navigation, but continuous phone use may distract visitors from artworks. Interactions should be short, optional, and available only when requested.

## 7. The Gallery Experience Can Continue After the Visit

Visitors may want to save meaningful artworks and personal responses. These saved items can become a record of the visit, allowing visitors to reflect, compare, and share their experiences afterwards.

# Design Opportunities

## 1. Support Low-Pressure Social Interaction

Create forms of interaction that do not require direct conversation. Visitors can anonymously and asynchronously encounter other people’s non-verbal responses and perspectives, reducing the social pressure of interacting with strangers.

## 2. Turn Personal Responses into Shared Content

Allow visitors to leave short feelings, observations, or interpretations connected to specific artworks. These personal responses can become content that other visitors can discover.

## 3. Represent Personal Interpretations Through Abstract Visual Forms

Transform visitors’ written responses into colours, shapes, lines, animations, or other abstract visual forms. This provides a lightweight way to express personal feelings and interpretations while reducing reliance on long written content.

## 4. Use Visitor Traces to Support Artwork Discovery

Present lightweight traces left by other visitors, such as feelings, observations, or visual responses. These traces can help visitors discover different artworks and perspectives without becoming compulsory recommendations.

## 5. Provide Optional Spatial Navigation

Use a gallery map to show visited, unvisited, and temporarily skipped areas. This can reduce repeated walking while preserving visitors’ freedom to choose their own routes.

## 6. Support Post-Visit Reflection and Sharing

Allow visitors to save favourite artworks, their own responses, and traces created by others. These saved items can form a personal collection that visitors can review and share after leaving the gallery.

## 7. Limit Digital Distraction from Artworks

Design interactions to be short, optional, and available on demand. Avoid continuous notifications, long reading tasks, and compulsory phone use so that visitors’ attention remains focused on the physical artworks.
