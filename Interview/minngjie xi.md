# Results and Findings

## People influence what others notice
We found that visitors often discover artworks through the behaviour of friends or other visitors. This suggests an opportunity for technology to make these social cues more visible and meaningful.

## Visitors want social connection without losing independence
We found that people enjoy sharing experiences, but they also want to explore at their own pace. Our technology should therefore support optional and lightweight interaction rather than requiring constant group participation.

## Conversation can deepen engagement with artworks
We found that discussing an artwork can help visitors look more closely, reflect, and consider different interpretations. A social technology could create small opportunities for visitors to share reactions, questions, or perspectives.

## Context matters during the gallery visit
We found that visitors’ needs change depending on where they are, who they are with, how crowded the space is, and what they are currently viewing. This means the technology should respond to the visitor’s immediate context rather than provide the same experience everywhere.

## Less experienced visitors may benefit from social guidance
We found that infrequent gallery visitors often use friends, crowds, and visible activity to decide what to explore. The technology could use contextual social information to help visitors discover artworks without telling them exactly what they should think.

## The technology should connect people through the gallery, not distract them from it
Our goal should not be to make visitors spend more time looking at a screen. Instead, the technology should encourage people to look back at the artwork, notice their surroundings, and engage with others in the space.

---

# Design Opportunity

> **How might we design a social and contextual technology that enhances gallery visitors’ awareness, discovery, and connection with others, while still supporting individual exploration and engagement with the artworks?**

## Participant 1
**Profile:** 26-year-old young professional in Brisbane; visits galleries several times a year, usually with friends or a partner.

**Interviewer:** Can you tell me about the last time you visited an art gallery? Who were you with, and what did you do during the visit?

**Participant 1:** The last time I went to an art gallery was with two friends. It was kind of a weekend activity. We wanted to catch up and thought going to an exhibition would be more interesting than just getting coffee.

**Interviewer:** So was the gallery itself the main reason you went, or was it more about spending time together?

**Participant 1:** I think it was a bit of both, but spending time together was definitely a big part of it. We all like going to interesting places, but none of us are really serious art people.

**Interviewer:** What did you actually do once you were inside?

**Participant 1:** We walked around together most of the time. We’d stop when one of us saw something interesting and talk about it a little. I also took a few photos, mostly of artworks that looked unusual or that I wanted to remember.

**Interviewer:** Did you follow a particular route through the gallery?

**Participant 1:** Not really. We were quite casual about it. Sometimes one person would walk ahead and then call the others over if they found something interesting.

**Interviewer:** Can you remember a moment when that happened?

**Participant 1:** Yeah. There was one large installation that I probably would have looked at for only a short time, but one of my friends noticed a small detail and pointed it out.

**Interviewer:** What happened after they pointed it out?

**Participant 1:** We started trying to work out what the different parts meant. We ended up standing there for quite a while because we were talking about it.

**Interviewer:** Do you think you would have spent as much time looking at it if your friend hadn’t pointed that detail out?

**Participant 1:** Probably not. I think I would have looked at it for a few seconds and then kept walking.

**Interviewer:** So did the conversation change the way you looked at the artwork?

**Participant 1:** Definitely. It made me look at it more carefully. Once we started talking about it, I noticed more things.

**Interviewer:** When you visit with friends or other people, does that usually affect what you pay attention to?

**Participant 1:** Yeah, definitely. When I’m with friends, I pay attention to what they’re interested in as well as what I’m interested in.

**Interviewer:** Can you give me an example?

**Participant 1:** Sometimes I might walk straight past something, but a friend stops and says, “Look at this.” Then I’ll come back and look at it with them.

**Interviewer:** Does being with other people ever have a negative effect on the experience?

**Participant 1:** Sometimes. I probably spend less time quietly reading all the information because I don’t want to make everyone wait for me.

**Interviewer:** Do you feel pressure to keep up with the group?

**Participant 1:** A little bit, yeah. If everyone else has already moved on and I’m still looking at something, I start thinking I should catch up.

**Interviewer:** What would you prefer in that situation?

**Participant 1:** I think it would be nice if we could separate for a little while and then meet again later. That way I could spend longer on something if I wanted to without feeling like I was holding everyone up.

**Interviewer:** Apart from the friends you were with, can you think of a time when another visitor changed what you noticed or did in the gallery?

**Participant 1:** Yeah. I remember seeing a few people gathered around one artwork, and they were looking at it from different angles.

**Interviewer:** What did you think when you saw them doing that?

**Participant 1:** At first, I didn’t know why, so I went over to see what they were looking at.

**Interviewer:** And what did you discover?

**Participant 1:** When I moved around it, I realised the artwork changed depending on where you stood.

**Interviewer:** Would you have discovered that without seeing the other visitors?

**Participant 1:** Probably not. I think I would have just looked at it from the front and moved on.

**Interviewer:** So do you think you pay attention to what other visitors are doing, even if you’re not talking to them?

**Participant 1:** Yeah, I think so. Not all the time, but if people seem really interested in something, it makes me curious about what they’re looking at.

**Interviewer:** Are there particular moments when you especially enjoy sharing the gallery experience with other people?

**Participant 1:** I like sharing it when there’s something surprising, funny, or confusing because it’s nice to immediately talk about it with someone.

**Interviewer:** Why is that important to you?

**Participant 1:** Sometimes my friends interpret things completely differently from me, and that makes it more interesting. I might think something means one thing, and they see it in a completely different way.

**Interviewer:** Do you need to agree with each other?

**Participant 1:** No, not really. I think disagreeing can actually make it more interesting because then you start looking at the artwork again and trying to understand why the other person sees it that way.

**Interviewer:** Are there also times when you would rather explore by yourself?

**Participant 1:** Yeah. Sometimes I want to look at something for longer and everyone else has already moved on. Then I feel a little pressure to catch up.

**Interviewer:** So being alone gives you something that being in a group doesn’t?

**Participant 1:** Yeah, mostly control over my own pace. If I’m alone, I can spend as much time as I want somewhere without worrying about anyone else.

**Interviewer:** Thinking back on the whole visit, what made the experience especially enjoyable?

**Participant 1:** The most enjoyable part was probably discovering things together and talking about them. It made the gallery feel less serious and more like something we were experiencing together.

**Interviewer:** Was there anything uncomfortable or tiring?

**Participant 1:** I did get a bit tired after walking and standing for a long time.

**Interviewer:** What did you do when you got tired?

**Participant 1:** We looked for somewhere to sit, but the available seating wasn’t always near the things we were interested in.

**Interviewer:** Would seating change the way you experience the gallery, or is it mainly just somewhere to rest?

**Participant 1:** I think it could be both. If there was somewhere to sit near an artwork, we could rest but also keep looking at it or talk about it together.

**Interviewer:** And what made the visit most memorable for you overall?

**Participant 1:** Probably the moments when something gave us a reason to stop, talk, or do something together rather than just walking from artwork to artwork.

**Interviewer:** If you could change one thing about the gallery experience to make those kinds of moments happen more often, what would you change?

**Participant 1:** Maybe something that encourages you to notice things or share what you think with the people you’re with, but without making you stop and use your phone all the time. I’d still want the main focus to be on the artwork and being there together.

## Participant 2
**Profile:** 34-year-old professional living in Brisbane; strong personal interest in art, exhibitions, architecture, and design; regularly visits galleries and often goes alone.

**Interviewer:** What do you like about visiting alone?

**Interviewer:** What did you do during that visit?

**Interviewer:** Was there a particular moment when something made you stop for longer?

**Participant 2:** Yeah. There was one work that didn’t really stand out to me initially, but after reading the short description beside it, I went back and looked again.

**Participant 2:** It gave me some context that I didn’t have before. I started noticing details in the work that I hadn’t really paid attention to at first.

**Interviewer:** How long did you stay there?

**Participant 2:** Probably five or ten minutes. I moved closer to look at some details and then stepped back again.

**Interviewer:** Would you have spent that long there if you had been visiting with someone else?

**Participant 2:** It would depend on who I was with. If they were interested too, maybe. But if they were already moving on, I’d probably feel a bit conscious about making them wait.

**Interviewer:** So being with someone can change the pace of your visit?

**Participant 2:** Definitely. When I’m with someone else, the visit becomes more conversational, but sometimes a little less focused.

**Interviewer:** Can you give me an example?

**Interviewer:** How did that make you feel?

**Participant 2:** A little rushed, I guess. Not in a really negative way, but I noticed that I wasn’t experiencing the exhibition in the way I normally would.

**Interviewer:** Is there anything positive about visiting with other people?

**Participant 2:** Absolutely. Sometimes they notice something I completely missed. Or they interpret something in a way I wouldn’t have thought of.

**Interviewer:** Can another visitor who you don’t know affect your experience as well?

**Participant 2:** Yeah. I remember overhearing someone quietly explaining an artwork to the person they were with. They mentioned something about the materials that I hadn’t noticed.

**Interviewer:** Did you listen to the whole conversation?

**Participant 2:** No, not really. I just caught part of it.

**Interviewer:** But that still changed what you did?

**Participant 2:** Yeah. It made me look at the work again. I suddenly noticed the material they were talking about, so even though I wasn’t part of their conversation, it influenced how I experienced the artwork.

**Interviewer:** Do other visitors ever have the opposite effect and make you move away from something?

**Participant 2:** Yes. If a group is talking very loudly or taking lots of photos in front of something, I sometimes move away and come back later.

**Interviewer:** Why do you do that?

**Participant 2:** I don’t necessarily want to compete for space, and I like having enough room to look properly. If it feels too busy, I find it harder to concentrate.

**Interviewer:** So does the number of people around an artwork change how long you stay there?

**Participant 2:** Definitely. If it’s crowded, I’ll probably spend less time there, even if I’m interested in the work.

**Interviewer:** When you do visit with someone, what kinds of moments do you enjoy sharing?

**Participant 2:** I enjoy discussing things with someone when we have similar interests, especially if we see the same work differently.

**Interviewer:** What do you get from those conversations?

**Participant 2:** A different perspective. Sometimes someone says something and I realise I hadn’t thought about the work in that way at all.

**Interviewer:** Do you prefer having that conversation while you’re standing in front of the artwork?

**Participant 2:** Sometimes, but I also really like talking about the exhibition afterwards. Sitting somewhere and talking about what we liked or didn’t like can be just as interesting.

**Interviewer:** If you visit with someone, do you feel like you need to stay together the whole time?

**Participant 2:** Not really. Actually, I’d prefer not to.

**Interviewer:** What would your ideal experience look like?

**Participant 2:** We might arrive together, explore separately for a while, and then meet up again. That way we both get to move at our own pace, but we can still share what we discovered.

**Interviewer:** Would you want some way of knowing what the other person found interesting while you were separated?

**Participant 2:** That could be useful, as long as it wasn’t distracting. If I could somehow see that they found something interesting and then decide whether I wanted to look at it too, that could be nice.

**Interviewer:** Thinking about the physical gallery space, was there anything that made the visit especially enjoyable?

**Participant 2:** I really enjoyed having quiet spaces where I could slow down without feeling like I was blocking someone else’s view.

**Interviewer:** Did seating make any difference?

**Participant 2:** Definitely. There was a bench positioned where I could actually look at one of the larger artworks, and I stayed there much longer than I probably would have if I’d had to keep standing.

**Interviewer:** So the seat became part of how you experienced the artwork?

**Participant 2:** Yeah, exactly. It wasn’t just somewhere to rest. It gave me a different way to look at the work.

**Interviewer:** Was there anything uncomfortable or tiring during the visit?

**Participant 2:** Mostly the crowded areas. I noticed that I changed my behaviour quite a lot. I’d spend less time there or skip something and return later.

**Interviewer:** What do you remember most about the visit overall?

**Participant 2:** Probably having the freedom to control the pace of the visit and decide when to stop, move, sit, or go back to something.

**Interviewer:** If you could add some kind of technology to support your gallery experience, what would you want it to do?

**Participant 2:** I’d want it to help me notice things or connect with other people’s perspectives without constantly demanding my attention. Maybe it could show that someone I’m with found something interesting, or let me see a short reaction from another visitor, but only when I choose to engage with it.

**Interviewer:** And what would you not want it to do?

**Participant 2:** I definitely wouldn’t want something that tells me exactly where to go or keeps giving me notifications. I’d still want to feel like I’m exploring the gallery myself.
