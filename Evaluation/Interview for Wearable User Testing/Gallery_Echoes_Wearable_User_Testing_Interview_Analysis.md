# Gallery Echoes Wearable Prototype Interview Analysis

## 1 Purpose and Summary

This analysis examines the available wearable prototype interview records to identify design issues concerning colour expression, physical feedback, wearability and palm posture, attention to artworks, social awareness and visitor control. The records support further exploration of brief colour responses and low-pressure social awareness, while identifying unresolved issues with explanation, controls and physical form. Preferences for phones, palm interaction and the “Look Again” prompt differ; the findings should not be presented as unanimous endorsement.

These findings are a qualitative analysis of existing written material. They are not a new user study, measured performance results or a retest of subsequent changes.

### 2.3 Conditions That Cannot Be Assumed Identical

W01–W05 discuss a palm interface, vibration and Echoes, while W06 and W07 more explicitly describe phone-based colour selection and physical light. The records do not consistently identify dates, software/hardware versions, settings, demonstration methods or whether each feature was physical, simulated or explained verbally. The analysis therefore identifies shared design concerns without assuming an identical implemented device across all records. Statements about vibration, collective artwork or “real-time” feedback are treated as reported experiences, not proof of implementation or performance.

## 4 Thematic Findings

### T1 Explain the Input–Output Relationship Before Adding Features

**Evidence:** C01, C02, C04; W02–W07.

W02 was unsure how choosing a colour created an Echo; W03 was uncertain about what followed opening the hand; W04 needed an initial instruction. W05 questioned whether an Echo represented emotion or opinion and wanted confirmation after acting. W06 understood after explanation. W07 understood more quickly after seeing phone and palm colours match, but remained unsure whether the light represented a personal colour or other visitors’ contributions. Visible physical feedback can help explanation without resolving the distinction between individual and collective output.

> W02: “I think the connection between choosing a color and creating an Echo was not immediately clear to me.”

> W05: “I would need a little more feedback after making a selection, so I know that my Echo has been saved.”

**Design implication:** Use a short introduction to explain choosing a personal colour, checking its light preview, confirming the contribution and optionally viewing others’ responses. Label previews, saving, sharing and examples distinctly. Confirmation must describe the action actually completed; local browser storage must not be presented as publication to a shared artwork.

### T2 Simple Colour Choice Has Value but Precision Can Distract

**Evidence:** C03, C08; W01–W07.

W01, W03, W04, W05 and W06 described colour selection as simple or smooth, and W07 found the phone-to-light relationship intuitive. However, W07 also reported looking away from the artwork while seeking an exact shade. W02 and W04 described distraction while learning the interface. Accessible colour input does not make the overall interaction free of attention costs.

> W07: “Choosing the exact shade on the phone was the most distracting part.”

Preferred durations differ: W02 suggested about 20–30 seconds; W03, 15–20 seconds; W04, under 20 seconds; W05, 10–15 seconds; W06, 3–5 seconds; and W07, about 15 seconds. These are stated preferences, not measured task times or evidence of one optimal duration.

**Design implication:** Keep a short path using preset colours and one confirmation, with detailed colour adjustment optional. Do not require a response at every artwork. Future testing should observe actual completion time, return to the artwork and skipping behaviour rather than only asking about preference.

### T3 Appealing Physical Feedback Does Not Establish a Suitable Wearable Form

**Evidence:** C05, C06, C07; W01–W07.

W01 and W05 described acceptable comfort; W02, W03 and W04 wanted a smaller or more stable form. W06 found the weight acceptable but preferred a phone and suggested a pendant or attached tag. W07 valued physical light in the palm while noting difficulty closing the hand, using a pocket and viewing both artwork and light comfortably. Comfort, ease of interaction and light visibility are separate concerns; low weight alone does not establish suitability for a full visit.

> W01: “Well, at first, it felt comfortable and quite light. I did not really notice it after wearing it for a while.”

> W04: “Holding my hand open for the interface felt a little awkward.”

W06 and W07 wanted softer light, with W06 explicitly considering disruption to other visitors. W07 preferred a distributed glow rather than a strong point of light. This supports testing diffusion, brightness and viewing angle, but the interviews do not prove that a later hardware modification was completed.

**Design implication:** Test light diffusion, visibility from a normal posture, hand freedom and alternative forms using physical prototypes. Retain a phone-based option instead of assuming palm interaction suits everyone.

### T4 Vibration Should Be a Controllable Cue Rather Than a Repeated Demand

**Evidence:** C09; W01–W07.

W01–W05 described potential value in vibration while repeatedly limiting its frequency. W06 suggested using it to confirm a recorded artwork but did not want repeated vibration during a quick visit. W07 described its possible reminder value while also finding it slightly unnecessary because the light already attracted attention. This conditional view within one record should not be reduced to endorsement.

> W03: “Helpful, but it should not happen too often.”

**Design implication:** Provide clear mute, ignore or quiet-mode controls and favour lightweight confirmation of user-initiated actions. Vibration in the current code is a screen preview; it must not be described as implemented physical haptics.

### T5 Social Awareness Has Value but Timing Affects Personal Interpretation

**Evidence:** C10, C11; W01–W07.

The records repeatedly express interest in encountering others’ responses through colours or Echoes. W03, W04, W06 and W07 described lower pressure than direct conversation. However, W02, W03 and W04 preferred forming a personal view before seeing others’ responses. W06 reported no influence on personal interpretation, whereas W07 described an additional perspective without a changed first impression. Social influence should not be presented as one consistent effect for everyone.

**Design implication:** Preserve space for a personal view by default while allowing voluntary early browsing without requiring submission. Label sample content clearly. Interview accounts of others’ contributions do not establish that cross-user sharing is implemented.

### T6 Look Again Should Be an Invitation Rather Than a Required Step

**Evidence:** C12; W03, W04, W05, W06, W07.

W03, W04, W05 and W07 described “Look Again” as helping return attention to the artwork or notice more detail. W06 explicitly disliked returning to something already seen. This counterexample shows that a reflective prompt can conflict with a visitor’s preferred pace.

> W06: “No. I do not really like going back to something I have already seen.”

**Design implication:** Make the prompt expandable and dismissible rather than a mandatory step or completion requirement. Observe continued viewing, skipping and leaving separately; skipping is not automatically failure.

### T7 Agency Includes Choosing Not to Use Additional Social Features

**Evidence:** C13, C14; W01–W07.

W02, W03 and W04 wanted clearer skipping controls. W05 wanted voluntary interaction without popularity numbers or rankings. W06 was neutral about skipping an Echo but valued choosing when to interact and questioned the usefulness of sketch uploads. W07 warned that choosing a colour at every artwork could become a checklist. Interest in colour responses should not be extended into demand for every social feature.

> W04: “Mostly, but I think the skip option should be more obvious.”

**Design implication:** Maintain a path that continues the visit without contribution. Keep sketches and likes optional and evaluate their usefulness and social pressure instead of treating usage volume as the sole success criterion.

## 6 Limitations and Research Ethics

- **Provenance and sample:** This analysis checks written files, not original recordings, recruitment information or participant mappings. The absence of fictional labels on files 01–07 does not authenticate them or establish seven distinct participants.
- **Versions and conditions:** Version, date and setting metadata are inconsistent, and interaction descriptions differ. The records do not establish a controlled comparison or pre/post test of one device.
- **Interview framing:** The guide presupposes vibration, palm interaction and Look Again, and some follow-ups are confirmatory. This can shape responses; a feature being discussed does not establish implementation.
- **Self-report:** Comfort, attention, absence of noticeable delay and willingness to use are subjective reports, not substitutes for extended wear, performance measurement or observed use.
- **Generalisation:** The small, incompletely described evidence set does not establish saturation, statistical representativeness or intercoder agreement. No success rates or population preferences are reported.
- **Public quotation:** Some records include recording consent, which is not publication consent. This file uses source identifiers without adding identity details; quotation permission and anonymisation still require team confirmation.

## 7 Recommendations for the Next Evaluation

1. **Identify the tested version and context.** Record software, firmware, physical form, simulated elements, date and setting.
2. **Observe unassisted use first.** Ask visitors to complete one response and explain their colour, light, saving and others’ examples.
3. **Check agency and attention.** Observe non-submission, skipping, light-off and return-to-artwork paths, including actual time and reasons.
4. **Verify physical and multi-user interaction separately.** Test natural posture, light diffusion and contribution flow between two visitors, identifying real and simulated behaviour.
5. **Update conclusions from new evidence.** Mark a response as validated only after actual retesting, retaining preferences against wearing, for phones or against additional social features.

## 8 Conclusion

The available records support retaining lightweight colour expression and optional access to others’ perspectives, alongside clearer explanation, lower attention demands and stronger visitor control. Physical light can make a phone action feel tangible, but form, posture and light diffusion still require evaluation of the final device. Subsequent work should connect findings, responses and validation through identified versions and actual retesting rather than presenting every suggestion as a completed outcome.
